Healthcare Patient Referral & Care Coordination Modernization

A healthcare provider network wants to modernize the end-to-end patient referral and care coordination capability without replacing its stable clinical core. The exercise focuses on incremental architecture, interoperability, responsible AI, privacy, resilience and measurable patient-care outcomes.

1. Exercise overview

Your team will take a healthcare-critical patient referral and care coordination capability from problem framing to a practical architecture recommendation. The scenario is intentionally realistic but generic so the thinking can be applied across provider networks, hospitals, clinics and specialty-care ecosystems.

Understand

Clarify the patient journey, operational pain points, constraints, assumptions and success measures.

Compare

Explore different modernization and interoperability approaches and their trade-offs.

Decide

Select a preferred direction and explain why it is safe, feasible and valuable.

Validate

Use a focused POC or experiment to test one important integration, workflow or AI idea.

Keep it practical. This is not a documentation competition. Use AI to accelerate analysis and creation, but keep clinical safety, privacy, architecture and final judgement with the team.

2. Architecture challenge

A regional healthcare provider network operates a business-critical patient referral and care coordination capability used by primary-care teams, specialists, care coordinators, patients and external partner providers.

The capability has grown over several years and now spans an electronic health record (EHR), referral portals, scheduling applications, laboratory and imaging systems, payer workflows, point-to-point interfaces, spreadsheets, email and manual coordination steps.

Current challenge

  • Referral information is distributed across multiple systems and hand-offs.
  • Referral status and ownership are not consistently visible end-to-end.
  • 15–20% of referrals require manual follow-up or rework.
  • Provider-to-provider integrations are tightly coupled and expensive to change.
  • Scheduling, eligibility and clinical context are not consistently available at the right step.
  • Rules and workflow knowledge are distributed across applications and teams.

Impact

  • Patients experience delays, duplicate calls and unclear next steps.
  • Care coordinators spend significant time chasing missing information.
  • Referral leakage and incomplete hand-offs are difficult to identify quickly.
  • New partner integrations and workflow changes take too long.
  • Operational and decision data are fragmented, limiting root-cause analysis.
  • The stable clinical core is expensive and risky to modify directly.
Architecture question: How should the healthcare provider modernize patient referral and care coordination incrementally while improving interoperability, patient visibility, scalability, resilience, maintainability, privacy, auditability and the responsible use of AI?
Healthcare lens: Treat clinical safety, minimum-necessary data, consent/privacy, auditability, human oversight and continuity of care as first-class architecture concerns. A referral may involve clinical notes, diagnoses, medications, insurance information and other sensitive data.

3. Constraints and success measures

Assume these constraints

  • The current EHR / clinical core cannot be replaced in Phase 1.
  • An initial usable capability is expected in about 12 weeks.
  • Normal patient-care and referral processing must continue during modernization.
  • Protected health information must be minimized, secured and auditable.
  • AI-assisted outputs must remain reviewable, explainable enough for their use and governed.
  • Interfaces should support interoperability standards and future partner channels.
  • Patient-facing automation must provide a safe path to human escalation.

Target outcomes

  • Reduce avoidable referral rework and manual follow-up.
  • Improve referral turnaround time and patient visibility.
  • Reduce referral leakage and incomplete hand-offs.
  • Make partner integrations and workflow changes easier.
  • Improve auditability, operational insight and exception management.
  • Create a safe path for future AI-enabled coordination capabilities.
Teams may add assumptions. State them clearly rather than silently inventing clinical, regulatory, technology or organizational facts.

4. What the team will do

Work through four practical activities. Do enough analysis to make a defendable healthcare architecture decision; do not create documentation that is not useful.

1

Frame

Map the patient/referral journey, stakeholders, problem, constraints, assumptions, safety considerations and success measures.

2

Compare & decide

Develop at least two credible architecture options, compare the main trade-offs and select a preferred direction.

3

Design

Create a simple target-state architecture showing the major capabilities, EHR and partner integrations, data boundaries, AI considerations and human-control points.

4

Validate & plan

Run one focused POC / experiment, then define a simple phased roadmap and the most important delivery, clinical-safety, privacy and technical risks.

7. A simple AI working model

Explore

Use AI to generate ideas, workflow alternatives and architecture patterns.

Challenge

Ask what is weak, missing, unsafe, biased, ungoverned or operationally risky.

Verify

Check important facts, standards, assumptions, security controls and evidence.

Own

The final recommendation, patient-safety posture and architecture decision are the team's.

Protect patient information. Use synthetic or organization-approved information and follow organizational privacy, security, clinical governance and AI/tool policies. Never use real patient-identifiable information in an unapproved AI tool.

8. POC / prototype / validation — keep it focused

The team chooses what to validate. The workbook does not prescribe screens, technology, implementation method or AI feature.

Ask one question: “What part of our proposed healthcare solution would benefit most from evidence before we recommend it?”

Choose one

A small interoperability flow, FHIR-style payload transformation, referral-routing experiment, workflow prototype, synthetic-data AI capability test, architecture spike, mock-up or other focused proof.

Time-box it

Aim for roughly 60–75 minutes of build / experiment effort.

Capture learning

State what you tested, what happened, what you learned, what safety/control questions remain and what is still unproven.

A full clinical application is not expected. A small proof that changes the team's confidence is enough. Prefer synthetic data and demonstrable architecture evidence.

10. Final submission

Submit the five deliverables as one concise healthcare architecture decision pack, plus any POC file/link or evidence needed for the demo.

Core pack

  • Patient journey & success snapshot
  • Architecture options & decision
  • Target-state architecture
  • Focused POC / validation evidence
  • Delivery, safety & risk plan

Keep it lightweight

  • Around 5–7 content pages/slides
  • One architecture diagram is enough
  • Top risks rather than a large risk register
  • Short AI-use reflection, not a prompt log
  • POC can be small if the learning is strong

Jury principle: Reward clear reasoning, patient-safe design, useful evidence and practical trade-offs. More documentation, more tools or a larger POC should not automatically score higher.

12. Final demonstration and jury challenge

Present the patient/problem context, options, decision, target state, POC learning, roadmap and top risks. Keep the prepared presentation short enough to leave time for discussion.

Possible jury changes

  • Referral volume increases significantly.
  • A proposed interoperability technology is unavailable in Phase 1.
  • AI cannot receive identifiable or sensitive health information.
  • The delivery timeline is reduced from 12 weeks.
  • A partner organization cannot support the preferred integration pattern.

Be ready to explain

  • Does your decision still hold?
  • What would you change first?
  • What did the POC actually prove?
  • How did you protect patient information?
  • Where did AI help and where did human judgement matter?
