Understanding the document

What the report describes

A healthcare network wants to improve how patients are referred between care teams. A referral may move through the electronic health record, portals, scheduling, diagnostic and insurance systems, external providers, emails and spreadsheets. Because information is scattered, people cannot consistently see the current status or who needs to act next.

The report recommends adding shared coordination around the existing systems. It also includes a working local application that tests selected referral-tracking rules.

Report page 1

D1 — Problem & success snapshot

Purpose: establish what needs improving and how improvement will be recognized before choosing a solution.

Problem statement

Referral information, status and responsibility are spread across different systems. The scenario reports that 15–20% of referrals require manual follow-up or rework. Patients can experience delays, repeated calls and uncertainty, while staff spend time checking status and finding missing details.

This figure is supplied by the scenario. Its referral group and reporting period still need validation; it is not a measurement from the POC.

People and current process

PeopleRoleDifficulty
PatientsReceive care and referral-related communication.Delays, repeated calls and unclear next steps.
Primary-care teamsInitiate referrals and supply relevant clinical context.Limited visibility after the referral leaves their team.
Care coordinatorsTrack progress, obtain missing details and coordinate hand-offs.Manual chasing across systems.
SpecialistsReceive referrals and provide specialist care.Required context may be unavailable when needed.
External partner providersExchange referrals and updates with the network.Inconsistent shared status and connections that are expensive to change.

Primary care initiates → information moves between systems → coordinators resolve gaps → specialist/partner acts → progress and outcome are shared.

The roles and process are a scenario-based summary. Clinical and operational owners must confirm them. Starting a referral does not establish that primary care owns the entire system.

Key constraints

  1. Retain the EHR: the electronic health record cannot be replaced in Phase 1.
  2. About 12 weeks: the first usable capability is expected within an approximate window, requiring a manageable scope.
  3. Continue care: changes must not depend on stopping normal patient care or referral processing.
  4. Protect PHI: use only necessary protected health information, secure it and record important actions.
  5. Govern AI and interfaces: any AI outputs need appropriate review; system connections must support suitable standards and future partners where feasible.
  6. Allow human escalation: patient-facing automation must provide a safe route to responsible staff.

Assumptions to validate

The plan depends on five unconfirmed conditions: a manageable first process can be selected; systems can exchange essential information; teams can agree IDs, status meanings and responsibilities; reliable starting measurements can be collected; and governance can define escalation and permitted AI use. If a condition fails, scope or design may need to change.

Except for the scenario's rework figure, numerical baselines are not yet measured and targets are to be agreed. These are intended improvements, not achieved results. Receipt, scheduling and completed care are distinct milestones.

Open questions and hand-off to D2

The report asks who handles urgent exceptions, which privacy/consent/retention rules apply, and what updates patients should receive. D2 must compare approaches against these needs while carrying unconfirmed scope, interfaces, roles and measures forward.

Report page 2

D2 — Architecture options & decision

Purpose: choose an approach that addresses D1's outcomes while respecting the constraints.

Decision context

Both options must preserve the EHR, care continuity, privacy and human escalation. Neither is assumed to have already reduced rework or improved turnaround. Interfaces, process ownership and measurement targets remain unresolved.

Option A — Shared coordination around the EHR

Add a capability that tracks referrals across systems, shows who acts next and directs problems to coordinators. Separate system connections isolate partner-specific changes.

The potential advantage is cross-organization visibility. The trade-off is another capability to operate, secure and keep consistent with existing systems.

Option B — Improve coordination within the EHR

Improve referral tracking and work queues mainly inside the EHR, connecting selected partners directly. Staff use the EHR as the main coordination workspace.

The potential advantage is fewer extra workspaces. The dependency is whether safe EHR changes and partner connections can provide sufficient coverage.

Trade-off comparison

The table compares the same criteria for both options: rework, turnaround/incomplete hand-offs, visibility, action history/privacy, initial delivery/care continuity, partner changes, and operating effort/growth.

A could expose problems across organizations if updates are reliable. B could improve internal work while external chasing remains. Neither option automatically solves delay: responsible people and partners still need to act. Existing EHR access and audit controls must also be assessed.

Preferred direction and accepted trade-offs

Option A is conditionally preferred because the stated problems cross system boundaries. It leaves clinical records in place while adding shared progress tracking.

The accepted costs are additional support work, another patient-information boundary to protect, and work to keep IDs, statuses and responsibilities consistent. “Conditionally” means the recommendation depends on discovery and approval; it is not an implementation commitment.

Dependencies and reasons to revisit the decision

  • Unavailable interfaces: narrow scope or reconsider a supported EHR-centered approach.
  • Unresolved roles or status meanings: defer shared coordination until agreement.
  • A demonstrably safer EHR change: reconsider B for the selected first process.
  • Unmet partner, privacy or escalation requirements: defer the affected flow and agree fallback.
  • Delivery threatens care continuity: reduce scope or revise sequencing.

Hand-off to D3

D3 must show shared tracking around the retained EHR, staff follow-up, connections, data boundaries and safe handling of failures. Interface choices, capacity and approvals remain validation items.

D3 — Target-state architecture

Purpose: describe how the selected approach would work, with clear responsibilities and boundaries.

Architecture view

Primary-care teams submit through Referral entry. Shared referral tracking maintains progress, next actions and history. System connections exchange permitted details and updates with the EHR and partner/supporting systems.

Two-way arrows represent information going out and updates returning. Coordinators receive problems and return reviewed actions. Patients would receive approved status/next-step information in the proposed target.

Major components and record ownership

ComponentResponsibility / boundary
Referral entryReceives in-scope referrals; does not replace clinical records.
Shared trackingTracks progress, next-action responsibility, follow-up tasks and history. Holds coordination data.
System connectionsExchange approved information and isolate system-specific changes. Connected systems retain their records.
Existing EHRContinues clinical documentation and remains authoritative for its clinical records.
Partner/supporting systemsContinue specialist, scheduling, diagnostic and insurance functions; exact record boundaries must be confirmed.

Integration and data

Actual interfaces, standards, mappings, patient/referral matching and partner participation need discovery. Store only the tracking information needed, such as identifiers, progress, responsibility and history. Missing updates never imply completion.

AI and human control

AI is optional and outside the core diagram. Any later use needs approved inputs and human review. Coordinators handle administrative follow-up and escalate clinical concerns to authorized clinicians; patients must have human help available.

Security and resilience

The design requires agreed access roles, consent, retention and protection of patient information. It also requires traceable actions, monitoring of delays/failures, accountable failure handling and safe recovery. Duplicate handling, retries, conflicting updates and fallback must be validated before pilot.

Design assumptions and alignment to D2

Clinical/operational owners must select the first process; technical/partner teams must verify connections and status meanings; privacy/security owners must confirm controls. Volume, recovery needs and feasible scope remain unproven.

This implements D2's conditional Option A and supports D1's measures. It does not establish that business improvements or controls are already effective.

This diagram is the proposed target. The local POC implements entry, tracking and staff follow-up; it does not implement real EHR/partner exchange or patient updates.

D4 — Focused POC / validation

Purpose: test one meaningful part of the architecture with observable software behavior.

Question and hypothesis

The question is whether local coordination can keep referrals visible through missing information, delayed receipt and human-approved retry while preserving records across refresh. The hypothesis is that correction, explicit tracking rules, saved deadlines and recorded human actions can support that workflow.

Scope and safety

The application covers referral entry, local tracking and coordinator follow-up. Records persist in the same browser; overdue status uses device time. Inputs must be fictional or approved non-identifying data. Person names are recorded labels, not authenticated identities.

There is no real EHR/partner transport, patient-facing communication, clinical decision logic or runtime AI.

Prototype behavior

  1. A missing specialty/provider blocks hand-off; staff can correct the details.
  2. Record hand-off stores a person and reference for an action performed externally. The application sends no message.
  3. A missing receipt becomes overdue after the saved deadline. Retry needs a person and reason; another hand-off is recorded separately.
  4. Record receipt stores the person's partner-confirmation reference. It does not independently verify delivery.
  5. Records/history survive refresh. Export creates a JSON copy; explicit reset clears the workspace and restores defaults.

The default follow-up is 30 minutes. It is a configurable local setting pending business agreement, not a clinical urgency rule or confirmed service target. Export has no automatic import/restore.

Test method and results

17 rule tests and 13 browser checks passed. Rule tests check the underlying workflow; browser checks exercise visible behavior. Timing checks use a controlled test clock, while the application uses actual device time.

Result groupWhat was established
Entry/correctionMissing details block hand-off; correction makes the same record usable.
References/deadlines/retryRequired person/reference information, overdue follow-up, reasoned retry and late receipt behavior.
Persistence/conflictsRefresh retains records; duplicate/conflicting entries preserve originals; stale tab writes are rejected.
Storage/resetFailed saves preserve data; cancel retains records; confirmed reset remains cleared.
Export/displayExport includes records/history/settings; search, filters and mobile layout work.

Results and learning

The evidence increases confidence in the local coordination rules. It supports the visibility, hand-off and action-record ideas in D1–D3. It does not demonstrate reduced rework, faster care, real delivery or representative-user acceptance.

Limitations and hand-off to D5

Browser storage is not protected central storage or a tamper-proof audit. Cleanup/reset can remove data. There are no authenticated users, enforced consent, real interfaces, patient matching or notifications. Capacity, operational recovery and clinical readiness are unproven. Device-clock errors affect deadlines; overdue status appears on reopening without closed-app alerts.

D5 therefore requires interface proof, accountable roles, appropriate storage/access/audit, recovery testing and safe escalation before operational pilot. D1 measures and targets still need agreement.

AI-use reflection

AI helped draft the pack, simplify the diagram, build the application and create automated checks. User review shaped the content and functionality. Software test results are separate from clinical/business approval. Clinical decisions, privacy acceptance, commitments and risk acceptance remain human responsibilities; no AI runs inside this application.

Delivery guardrails

Keep the EHR and normal care in place; retain clinical records in source systems; use only necessary approved information; and preserve human escalation. Do not mistake browser persistence for protected storage, typed labels for authenticated identity, recorded receipt for verified delivery, or export for tested restoration.
