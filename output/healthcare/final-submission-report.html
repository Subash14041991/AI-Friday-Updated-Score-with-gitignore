D1. Problem & success snapshot

Problem, people, constraints, assumptions, and measurable outcomes — based on the supplied healthcare scenario.

Problem statement

Referrals span the electronic health record (EHR), portals, scheduling, diagnostic/insurance systems, partners, spreadsheets, and emails. Staff cannot consistently see progress or who acts next. The scenario reports 15–20% of referrals require manual follow-up or rework. Patients face delays, repeated calls, and unclear next steps; coordinators chase missing information. The goal is clearer tracking and reliable hand-offs while preserving the EHR, care, privacy, and human oversight.

Key constraints

  • Keep the EHR in Phase 1; an initial usable capability is expected in about 12 weeks.
  • Continue normal patient care and referral processing during modernization.
  • Minimize and secure protected health information (PHI); keep actions traceable.
  • Govern AI-assisted outputs; keep them reviewable and sufficiently explainable.
  • Support interoperability standards and future partners; confirm available interfaces.
  • Give patient-facing automation a safe route to a person for escalation.

  • Which consent, access, retention, and audit rules apply?
  • What information and next-step updates should patients receive?

D2. Architecture options & decision

Decision context

The network needs fewer referral corrections and delays, clearer status and responsibility, fewer incomplete hand-offs, and more complete action records. Keep the electronic health record (EHR), continue patient care, protect patient information, and aim for an initial usable capability in about 12 weeks. D1's interfaces, process ownership, starting measurements, and targets remain unconfirmed. Compare two approaches; neither has demonstrated these improvements yet.

Option A — Shared referral coordination around the EHR

Add a shared capability that tracks referral progress across systems, shows who acts next, and directs problems to care coordinators. Connect it to the EHR and selected partners through replaceable connections. Clinical records stay in the EHR; the new capability tracks coordination.

Potential benefit: A shared view could reduce chasing and reveal delayed or incomplete hand-offs across organizations. Separate connections could make partner changes easier.

Main trade-off / dependency: Another capability must be operated and protected. Teams must agree identifiers, status meanings, responsibilities, and how conflicting updates are resolved. Usable interfaces and partner participation are prerequisites.

Option B — Improve referral coordination within the EHR

Improve referral tracking and work queues mainly inside the existing EHR, connecting selected partners directly. Staff use the EHR as the main coordination workspace; external providers retain their own systems.

Potential benefit: Could improve internal referral work while keeping staff close to the clinical record and limiting additional workspaces.

Main trade-off / dependency: The scenario says modifying the clinical core is expensive and risky. Feasibility depends on supported configuration and interfaces; partner-specific connections may preserve existing change difficulties. Existing access and audit controls still need assessment.

Preferred direction

Conditionally prefer Option A for one agreed referral process. It more directly addresses D1's cross-system visibility, missing hand-offs, and manual chasing, while retaining the EHR. It does not guarantee faster referrals or lower rework.

Accepted trade-offs:

  • Another capability to operate and support.
  • Another patient-information boundary to protect and audit.
  • Work to keep referral identities, statuses, and responsibilities consistent.

Keep clinical authority with authorized staff, preserve patient-care continuity, and provide human escalation. AI is optional and requires approved data use and review.

Decision dependencies and revisit triggers

  • Interfaces unavailable: narrow the first process or reconsider a supported EHR-centered approach.
  • Roles or statuses unresolved: defer shared coordination until clinical and operational owners agree them.
  • EHR changes demonstrably safer for this scope: reconsider Option B for Phase 1.
  • Partner, privacy, or escalation requirements unmet: defer the affected flow and agree a safe fallback.
  • 12-week scope threatens care continuity: reduce scope or revise sequencing.

Handoff to D3

  • Show the retained EHR, shared tracking, coordinator tasks, partner connections, and clinical versus coordination data boundaries.
  • Show access, audit, failure handling, and human escalation; validate actual interfaces and standards support before selecting mechanisms.
  • Carry D1's measures and unresolved scope, ownership, privacy, and capacity questions into design validation.

Integration and data

  • Exchange uses supported interfaces; standards, mappings, identity matching, and partner participation need validation. Specialist teams act through their existing systems/channels; exact channels need confirmation.
  • Store only necessary identifiers, progress, next-action responsibility, and history. Receipt, scheduling, and completed care are separate milestones; absent updates never mean completion.

Alignment to D2

This implements D2's conditional Option A around the retained EHR. Shared tracking and follow-up tasks support D1's rework, turnaround, visibility, hand-off, and action-record measures; improvement must be measured, not assumed.

AI and human control

AI is optional and outside the core diagram. Any later use requires approved inputs and human review; no autonomous clinical decisions. Coordinators resolve administrative problems and escalate clinical concerns to authorized clinicians. Patients must have a route to human help.

Security and resilience

  • Confirm access roles, consent, retention, and protection of patient information in storage and exchange.
  • Record attributable actions; monitor delayed referrals and failed connections using minimum necessary data.
  • Give failures a responsible person and agreed fallback. Validate duplicate handling, safe retry, conflicting updates, and recovery before pilot; normal care must continue.

Design assumptions and validation points

  • Clinical and operational owners must select the first referral process and confirm responsibilities; approval is not yet recorded.
  • Technical and partner teams must verify interfaces, identifiers, status meanings, and data boundaries.
  • Privacy/security owners must confirm controls and patient communication rules.
  • Validate expected volume, recovery needs, and feasible scope against the approximately 12-week initial capability expectation; capacity and resilience are unproven.

D4. Focused POC / validation

Working local coordination application · Verified behavior, learning, and limits.

Question and hypothesis

Question: Can local coordination keep referrals visible through missing information, delayed receipts, and human-approved retry, while preserving records across refresh?

Hypothesis: Staff can correct incomplete entries, record external hand-offs and receipt references, see overdue follow-up from saved deadlines, and retain attributable action history in the same browser.

Scope and safety

The working local application covers D3's referral entry, shared tracking, and staff follow-up. Records persist in browser storage; deadlines use device time. Partner/EHR exchange and patient updates are excluded. No clinical decisions or runtime AI. Use fictional or approved non-identifying data; typed names are recorded labels, not authenticated identities.

Prototype

Open Referral coordination; keep its four supporting files together. See the usage and practice guide.

  1. Add a referral with missing specialty; correct it through Edit details.
  2. Record hand-off with a person/reference from an external action; the application sends no messages.
  3. Set a short follow-up before a new hand-off, wait for its deadline, and approve retry with a person/reason. Record the next external hand-off separately.
  4. Record receipt with partner confirmation; inspect history and refresh to verify persistence.
  5. Export if needed. Reset requires typing RESET; it clears local records/history and restores defaults. Export has no automatic import/restore.

Receipt is not completed care. The default follow-up is 30 minutes, a configurable local setting pending business agreement.

Test method

Run tests/test_coordination_workspace.cjs and tests/validate_coordination_browser.cjs with the bundled Node runtime. Timing tests use a controlled clock; the application itself uses real time and has no time-advance control.

Results and learning

17 rule tests and 13 browser checks passed. Engine run: 08 Oct 2026, 10:35 AM IST; browser run: 08 Oct 2026, 10:36 AM IST. Rule evidence and browser evidence match the current source hashes.

Results support D2's coordination concept and D1's visibility, hand-off, and action-record measures. They show local software behavior, not reduced rework, faster care, verified partner delivery, or representative-user usability.

Limitations and what remains unproven

  • Browser persistence is not protected central storage or a tamper-proof audit. Cleanup/reset can remove data; export is not a tested restore.
  • No authenticated users, enforced consent, clinical validation, real interfaces, patient matching, or patient notifications. Recorded receipt is a person's statement.
  • No production capacity, outage recovery, business-outcome or AI-performance evidence. Device time affects deadlines; overdue status appears on reopening, without closed-app alerts.

Handoff to D5

Require real interface proof, accountable roles, appropriate storage/access/audit, recovery testing, and safe escalation before an operational pilot. Use D1 baselines and agreed targets; D2 remains conditional.
